I. Origin Story: The Goodreads for Film

Letterboxd was created by two New Zealanders, Matthew Buchanan and Karl von Randow, in 2011. The founding impulse was charmingly simple. Buchanan used Flickr for photos and Last.fm for music. He wanted the equivalent for film — a social layer on top of the watching, a place where your taste could live and breathe and be seen.

IMDb existed, sure, but its interface was never social in any meaningful sense. So they built what they called, without too much modesty, the Goodreads for movies.

In 2020, the app had 1.8 million users. By 2025, it reported 17 million worldwide.

The company is even named after a film technique — letterboxing — the practice of preserving a film's original widescreen aspect ratio when converting it to a narrower format. So the name alone is a love letter to cinema's formal integrity. That's rather sweet.

And then the pandemic happened. Among those millions: Charli XCX, Martin Scorsese, Ed Sheeran, and a disproportionately enormous contingent of Generation Z. What started as cinema's answer to Goodreads had become, as one writer put it, "a redoubtable force in shaping film culture's future."

The niche had swallowed the mainstream. Letterboxd partnered with the Oscars in 2023, Tom Cruise spoke into their microphone, and Greta Gerwig met with their team. Two years before that, nobody was returning their calls.

So here is the question worth sitting with: what did it mean when cinephilia went mass-market? What happens to the love of film when it gets a follower count?

II. The Promise — and the Poison Inside It

Let me tell you what Letterboxd sells you. It sells you community. Discovery. The radical democratisation of taste. Unlike a legacy site like Rotten Tomatoes, which gives more credence and airtime to professional critics, Letterboxd democratises reviewing and makes it entirely public.

A review of Todd Haynes' Carol that reads "all i want for christmas is a milf" has the same algorithmic chance of being seen by thousands as a 2,000-word essay on the Freudian death drive and its relationship to queer desire in 1950s melodrama. Traditional critics still matter — but they no longer own the conversation. Letterboxd users shape taste through humour, virality, aesthetics, and community-driven recommendation loops.

And honestly? Some of that is genuinely exciting. If Rotten Tomatoes has become a tool of Hollywood's homogenising marketing machinery, Letterboxd is something else: a cinephilic hive buzzing with authentic enthusiasm and heterogeneous tastes. You can find accounts like Solidarity Cinema, whose vast catalogue of leftist, radical and queer films functions as a community-access index.

The philosopher C. Thi Nguyen has a concept called value capture. The idea is this: you have a rich, complicated, somewhat ineffable set of values — in this case, your love of cinema, your aesthetic sensibility, whatever makes you you as a viewer. Then someone designs a system to measure those values. Star ratings. Follower counts. Number of films logged.

And here is the insidious thing: the measurement system "simplifies and distorts what you really care about." Over time, you stop optimising for the values themselves. You start optimising for the metrics. The map eats the territory.

Matthew Strohl, a philosopher of art, applies this directly to Letterboxd in his book Difficult to Watch. He writes that it becomes easy to start treating the stats as ends in themselves — leading people to change their viewing strategies just to boost their score.

"Increasing my score on Letterboxd is a terrible reason to watch a movie... But if it were December 31st and I was sitting at 999 films for the year, I'd absolutely squeeze one more in. Value capture is real."

III. The Performing Cinephile

There are videos online that depict what's been called the "Post-Credit Reflex" — where users instantly log a movie after it finishes, rather than sit with the feeling that the movie provokes. Think about what that means. The liminal space at the end of a film — that suspended, strange, open moment where the thing you just experienced is still working on you — is interrupted by the compulsion to rate and quip before it can do its job.

The aesthetic experience is cut short by the administrative act of processing it.

The key word there is performing. When you watch a film and then go on Letterboxd to rate it, you might lower your rating because the average is low and the reviews are unfavourable. Or raise it for the opposite reason. The pressure, many users report, is almost universal. You don't lose your own reaction entirely — but it gets distorted.

And this connects to something Pierre Bourdieu understood very well. Taste is never just private. Taste is social. It is one of the primary tools through which we perform class, signal cultural capital, locate ourselves in a hierarchy. On Letterboxd, this becomes especially visible through diary entries and public ratings, where engagement is no longer private but publicly visible.

The "Top Four" films on your Letterboxd profile is the perfect symbol of this dynamic. Choosing four favourites very quickly becomes an exercise in curating a particular collection of films that you wish to represent something about yourself, rather than a definitive list of the very best films you have actually loved.

There was discussion when the 2022 Sight & Sound poll was released surrounding director Ti West's ten film choices. Users deemed them too basic, too generic — a list that included Citizen Kane, 2001: A Space Odyssey, Jaws, and The Godfather. All virtually universally considered masterpieces. And yet the Letterboxd generation objected to West's lack of individualism, the subtext being his failure to choose something obscure enough.

Think about the logic of that objection for a moment. A professional filmmaker listing Jaws as one of the greatest films he has ever seen is considered insufficient — not because Jaws is bad, but because it is too known.

"The value being performed here is not aesthetic discernment. It is the performance of aesthetic discernment. The signal matters more than the thing being signalled."

IV. What the Algorithm Rewards

Strohl is pointed on what Letterboxd's social mechanics actually incentivise. The most popular reviews, he observes, tend to be either ecstatic one-liners — "MASTERPIECE" with several flame emoji — or pithy takedowns of things people enjoy hating.

Now, I want to be careful here, because there's a class politics lurking in the dismissal of the one-liner. "Emotionally devastating but in a hot way" as a review is not worse than 2,000 words of Lacanian apparatus — it's differently calibrated, speaking to a different sensibility, making different claims.

The problem isn't the wit. The problem is that the system rewards the wit specifically because it is frictionless and shareable, and punishes the patience required for anything more demanding. That's not democratisation. That's the algorithm doing what algorithms do: optimising for engagement, which is not the same thing as truth, or beauty, or care.

V. The Dialectical Turn — What Can Be Saved

I said this was going to be a critical analysis. But criticism that refuses to hold complexity is just noise in a different key. So let me try to hold the contradiction.

Letterboxd is also genuinely, meaningfully good. It democratises film criticism, bringing a wide array of opinions representing diverse backgrounds and viewpoints. For people — particularly queer people, people from the Global South, people whose taste has never been legible to the prestige critical establishment — the ability to find community around films that speak to your experience without requiring the permission of a gatekeeper is not trivial. It is, in some cases, transformative.

Documenting your thoughts after a film better consolidates it into memory and encourages genuine reflection on your aesthetic sensibility. The review as a tool of self-knowledge, not self-performance — that is a real and valuable thing. The problem is that the platform's social architecture consistently pulls you away from the former and toward the latter.

VI. So What Do We Do With This?

The problem with Letterboxd is not that it exists. The problem is that it reproduces, faithfully and efficiently, the logic of every other platform economy: convert an intrinsic good (the love of film) into an extrinsic performance (the accumulation of cultural capital and social metrics), and then make that conversion feel so natural that you forget an alternative was possible.

The corrective isn't to delete the app. It's to become a much more deliberate user of it. Write reviews before reading anyone else's. Log films after sitting with them. Resist the résumé.

And perhaps most importantly: recognise that the silence after the credits is not dead time waiting to be filled with a notification.

It is the film still happening inside you.
It would be a shame to interrupt it.
The question Letterboxd poses—and refuses to answer—is the oldest question in aesthetics:
What is art actually for?
Not for logging. Not for rating. Not for likes.
If it's for anything at all, it's for that unbearable, irreducible moment when something made by another human being makes you feel slightly less alone.
